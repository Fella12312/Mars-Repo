ERAS OF EXPLORATION

1964 – 1971
The Mariner Era

NASA's earliest Mars missions used the Mariner spacecraft to conduct flybys and orbital surveys. Mariner 4 transmitted the first close-up images of Mars in 1965 — 21 grainy frames that revealed a barren, cratered world and shattered hopes of a life-rich planet. Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft to orbit another planet.

1975 – 1980
The Viking Era

Viking 1 and Viking 2 each consisted of an orbiter and a lander. They were the first spacecraft to successfully land on Mars and return data from the surface. Their biology experiments searched for life — the results remain debated to this day. Viking 1's lander operated for over 6 years, a record at the time.

1992 – 2001
Reconnection & Rovers

After a 17-year gap following Viking, NASA returned with Mars Observer (lost before arrival), Mars Global Surveyor (a landmark success), and Mars Pathfinder — which deployed Sojourner, the first Mars rover. This era also saw the catastrophic loss of the Mars Climate Orbiter due to a unit-conversion error.

2001 – 2013
The Golden Age

NASA's most prolific period at Mars. Mars Odyssey (still operating), the MER rovers Spirit and Opportunity, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, and the Phoenix lander all launched during this window. Opportunity drove for 45 km over 15 years. MRO's HiRISE camera can resolve objects as small as a dinner table from orbit.

2013 – Present
Science & Sample Return

MAVEN studies the Martian atmosphere, InSight listened to Marsquakes, and Perseverance is collecting rock cores for eventual return to Earth — humanity's most ambitious interplanetary sample collection campaign. The Mars Sample Return mission will attempt to bring these samples home in the 2030s.

MISSION LOG

1964
Mariner 3 & 4
FLYBY SPACECRAFT
Partial +

Mariner 3 was lost when its launch shroud failed to jettison. Mariner 4 succeeded, becoming the first spacecraft to fly past Mars and return images. Its 21 photographs — transmitted over 8.5 hours at 8.3 bits per second — showed a cratered, Moon-like surface and an atmosphere far thinner than expected. The images shattered the popular image of Mars as a habitable world.

Closest Approach: 9,846 km Images Returned: 21 Launch Vehicle: Atlas Agena-D
1969
Mariner 6 & 7
FLYBY SPACECRAFT
Success +

Mariner 6 and 7 flew past Mars within days of each other, returning 201 images between them. They studied the Martian atmosphere and surface composition, finding carbon dioxide ice at the south polar cap. The missions confirmed Mars had a thin, cold atmosphere and no magnetic field, further dampening hopes for surface life.

Images Returned: 201 Flyby Speed: ~3.3 km/s South Polar Cap: CO₂ Ice Confirmed
1971
Mariner 9
ORBITER
Success +

Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft ever to orbit another planet, arriving in November 1971 in the midst of a planet-wide dust storm. It waited for the storm to clear, then mapped 85% of the Martian surface. It revealed Olympus Mons, Valles Marineris, and river channels suggesting ancient flowing water.

Images: 7,329 Surface Mapped: 85% Orbital Period: ~12 hours Discoveries: Olympus Mons, Valles Marineris
1975
Viking 1 & 2
ORBITER + LANDER
Success +

The Viking program was NASA's most ambitious Mars effort of the 20th century. Viking 1 landed on July 20, 1976 — the 7th anniversary of Apollo 11 — becoming the first spacecraft to successfully operate on the Martian surface. Both landers conducted biology experiments searching for signs of life; the results have never been fully explained. Viking 1's lander operated for 6 years and 116 days.

Viking 1 Landing: July 20, 1976 Viking 2 Landing: September 3, 1976 Biology Experiments: 4 (results inconclusive) Total Images: ~55,000
1992
Mars Observer
ORBITER
Failed +

Mars Observer was NASA's first Mars mission in 17 years. Contact was lost on August 21, 1993, just three days before it was due to enter orbit. A review board concluded a rupture likely occurred in the fuel pressurization system, sending the spacecraft into an uncontrolled spin. The $813 million loss triggered a major shift toward NASA's "faster, better, cheaper" mission philosophy.

Cost: $813 million Contact Lost: Aug 21, 1993 Cause: Fuel system rupture (probable)
1996
Mars Global Surveyor
ORBITER
Success +

One of the most successful interplanetary missions ever flown. MGS operated for nearly 10 years, mapping the entire planet, measuring its magnetic field, tracking dust storms, and imaging surface changes over time. Its camera captured evidence of recent gully erosion possibly carved by liquid water. MGS returned more data about Mars than all previous missions combined.

Operational Life: ~10 years Images: ~240,000 Key Finding: Recent gullies (possible water)
1996
Mars Pathfinder + Sojourner
LANDER + ROVER
Success +

Mars Pathfinder landed using a novel airbag system on July 4, 1997, and deployed Sojourner — the first rover on Mars. Though designed for a 7-day mission, Sojourner operated for 83 sols. It analysed 16 rocks and took 550 images. The mission's live webcam drove unprecedented public interest, receiving 47 million internet hits on its first day.

Landing: July 4, 1997 Rover Operational: 83 sols Rocks Analysed: 16 Cost: $265 million
1998
Mars Climate Orbiter
ORBITER
Failed +

One of spaceflight's most embarrassing losses. MCO was destroyed when it entered the Martian atmosphere at the wrong angle because one engineering team used metric units while another used imperial units for thruster data. The spacecraft entered the atmosphere 100 km too low and disintegrated. The $327 million mission lasted less than 10 months.

Cost: $327 million Cause: Metric/imperial unit mismatch Lost: September 23, 1999
2001
Mars Odyssey
ORBITER — ACTIVE
Active +

Mars Odyssey is the longest-operating spacecraft ever sent to Mars. Launched in April 2001 and still operational today, it has mapped hydrogen — a proxy for water ice — across the entire planet, detected thermal signatures of minerals, and served as a critical communications relay for surface missions. Its THEMIS camera has returned over 1 million images.

Launch: April 7, 2001 Status: Active (2025) Key Discovery: Hydrogen/ice distribution Role: Relay for surface missions
2003
Spirit & Opportunity (MER)
ROVERS
Success +

The Mars Exploration Rovers were designed for 90-day missions. Spirit survived 2,208 sols; Opportunity drove 45.16 km over 5,498 sols. Together they confirmed Mars once had liquid water on its surface. Opportunity found hematite "blueberries" formed in standing water. Spirit discovered silica-rich soil suggesting hydrothermal activity.

Spirit Operated: 2,208 sols Opportunity Drove: 45.16 km Opportunity Operated: 5,498 sols Key Finding: Confirmed ancient liquid water
2005
Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter
ORBITER — ACTIVE
Active +

MRO is the most powerful remote sensing spacecraft ever sent to another planet. Its HiRISE camera captures surface details as small as 25 cm from 300 km altitude. CRISM has mapped mineral deposits across Mars, and SHARAD has used radar to peer beneath the surface. MRO has returned more data than all previous interplanetary missions combined.

Launch: August 12, 2005 HiRISE Resolution: ~25 cm/pixel Data Returned: >450 terabits Status: Active (2025)
2007
Phoenix Mars Lander
LANDER
Success +

Phoenix landed in the arctic region of Mars to study the history of water in the soil. Its robotic arm excavated trenches and delivered soil samples to onboard instruments. Phoenix directly confirmed the presence of water ice just below the Martian surface — watching it sublimate in sunlight. It also discovered perchlorate salts in the soil.

Landing Site: 68.22°N (Arctic) Operated: 157 sols Confirmed: Subsurface water ice Found: Perchlorate salts
2011
Curiosity (MSL)
ROVER — ACTIVE
Active +

The Mars Science Laboratory mission delivered Curiosity — the size of a car — to Gale Crater using a revolutionary sky crane landing system. Curiosity confirmed that Gale Crater was once a habitable lake environment, detected organic molecules in ancient rock, and tracked seasonal methane variations. It is still driving today, climbing the slopes of Mount Sharp.

Landing: August 6, 2012 Power: Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator Distance Driven: >30 km Status: Active (2025)
2013
MAVEN
ORBITER — ACTIVE
Active +

The Mars Atmosphere and Volatile EvolutioN mission investigates how Mars lost its thick atmosphere billions of years ago. MAVEN found that solar wind strips about 100 grams of atmosphere per second from Mars — transforming a potentially warm, wet world into the frozen desert it is today. It also serves as a communications relay for surface rovers.

Launch: November 18, 2013 Finding: ~100g atmosphere lost per second Status: Active relay + science (2025)
2018
InSight
LANDER
Success +

InSight studied Mars' interior — the first mission to do so. Its seismometer detected hundreds of Marsquakes, revealing Mars has a surprisingly large liquid core and a thicker crust than expected. The mission ended in December 2022 when dust accumulation reduced its solar power below operational thresholds.

Landing: November 26, 2018 Marsquakes Detected: >1,300 Largest Quake: Magnitude 5 End of Mission: December 21, 2022
2020
Perseverance + Ingenuity
ROVER + HELICOPTER — ACTIVE
Active +

Perseverance is NASA's most technologically advanced rover, landing in Jezero Crater on February 18, 2021. It carries the MOXIE instrument which produced breathable oxygen from the Martian atmosphere, collected rock core samples for future Earth return, and deployed the Ingenuity helicopter. Ingenuity completed 72 flights before its blades were damaged in early 2024.

Landing: February 18, 2021 MOXIE: Oxygen production confirmed Ingenuity Flights: 72 completed Samples Cached: 23+ cores Status: Active (2025)

FUTURE MISSIONS

Planned
Mars Sample Return

A joint NASA/ESA campaign to retrieve the rock cores cached by Perseverance and return them to Earth. The most complex interplanetary mission ever attempted — involving a Sample Retrieval Lander, an Earth Return Orbiter, and the first rocket ever launched from another planet's surface.

TARGET: 2030s
Concept
Mars Ice Mapper

A proposed orbiter to precisely map subsurface water ice deposits accessible near the Martian surface — critical intelligence for selecting future human landing sites. Ice within 1–2 metres of the surface could be mined for water, rocket propellant, and radiation shielding.

TBD
Human Exploration
Crewed Mars Mission

Both NASA and SpaceX have outlined plans for eventual human missions to Mars. NASA's Moon to Mars architecture envisions crewed Mars expeditions in the late 2030s to 2040s, building on experience from the Artemis lunar program. A crewed Mars mission would involve transit times of 6–9 months each way, surface stays of up to 500 days, and radiation exposure management across the entire mission.

TARGET: LATE 2030s
